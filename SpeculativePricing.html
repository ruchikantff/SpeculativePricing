    1. What we do in speculative pricing

    Speculative pricing means we sell a ticket before we own it. We advertise tickets on secondary markets (marketplaces where people resell tickets, such as SkyBox / Vivid Seats). Only after a customer buys do we go and buy the real ticket. Our profit is the gap between what the customer pays and what we pay.

    The cycle, step by step

    • Step 1, List: we post tickets for events on the secondary markets. We hold no tickets at this point; the listing is a promise to deliver.
    • Step 2, Price: we choose the price from three things: what the market is charging, what we estimate the ticket will cost us, and the profit margin we want.
    • Step 3, Sale: a customer buys the listing. Now the risk is live: we must deliver.
    • Step 4, Fulfil: we buy the ticket on the primary market (the official seller, e.g. Ticketmaster), if it is still available.
    • Step 5, Deliver: we send the ticket to the customer.
    • Step 6, Profit: we keep sale price minus what we actually paid (the realised cost).

    Example 1: we list a ticket at $110 with an estimated cost of $41.79. It sells, we buy it for $41.79 and keep 110 − 41.79 = $68.21. If it never sells we spend nothing and lose nothing.

    Example 2 (event ticket): 8 GA tickets for Jon McLaughlin & Eric Hutchinson at the 04 Center, Austin (Fri, Jan 17) are listed at $110. One pair sells. We buy those tickets on the primary market at about $41.79 each, deliver them, and the other tickets stay unbought, so they carry no risk.

    What can go wrong

    • Underestimated cost: the real price on the primary market is higher than we assumed, so the profit shrinks or turns into a loss. Example: we assumed $29.36 and the real price is $34.00, which costs us $4.64 per ticket.
    • Primary sell-out: the primary market has no tickets left, so we cannot fulfil the order at all.

    2. Regular pricing vs speculative pricing (every difference explained)

    TopicRegular pricingSpeculative pricingWhat it means in practice
    ApproachBuy low, sell highSell high, buy lowRegular: you buy first, then look for a buyer. Speculative: you find the buyer first, then buy.
    Main riskLoss from a bad marketLoss from underestimated cost; risk of primary sell-outRegular: you paid money and prices fall. Speculative: you promised a sale and the cost turns out higher, or the ticket is gone.
    InventoryActual inventory (you own tickets)No inventoryRegular ties up cash in tickets. Speculative uses no cash until a sale happens.
    Expiry riskYes, unsold tickets are worth nothing after the eventNo, nothing to expireRegular can lose the whole purchase price. Speculative cannot lose on tickets it never bought.
    MarginsLowerHigherRegular accepts small profits to move stock quickly. Speculative asks for more per sale because each sale carries cost and sell-out risk.
    Sales frequencyMore frequent salesLess frequent salesLower prices attract more buyers; higher margins attract fewer.
    Inventory turnoverHigherLowerRegular cycles stock fast. Speculative sells less often, so it earns more on each sale.

    Example 1 (regular): you buy a ticket for $50 and list it at $70. The show happens and it never sold, so you lose the $50.

    Example 2 (speculative, event ticket): we list an Alan Jackson parking pass (T-Mobile Center, Kansas City) at $55 with an estimated cost of $29.36. If nobody buys it, we lose nothing. If it sells but Ticketmaster has since raised the price to $60, we lose $5. If the lot sells out, we cannot deliver.

    3. Terminology (each term with two examples)

    Cost

    The estimated cost of buying the ticket on the primary market. It is an estimate, because the real price is only paid after the sale.

    Why it matters: Every margin is looked up from the cost, so a wrong cost gives a wrong price.

    Example 1: a ticket whose face price plus fees comes to about $35 has a cost of $35.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Joshua Radin, 04 Center, Austin, GA: cost $47.22. Dave Barnes GA: cost $30.00.

    Margin

    The minimum profit, in dollars (not a percentage), that we want on a ticket of a given cost. It comes from the margin chart.

    Why it matters: A $35 ticket and a $75 ticket do not get the same profit. The chart sets a fair minimum for each range.

    Example 1: cost $35 → $27 profit; cost $75 → $53; cost $113 → $80.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Dave Barnes GA, cost $30.00 falls in $21–$30.99, so the margin is $20.

    B-Price

    The input column in SkyBox. It is the price of the listing before SkyBox takes its percentage. This is the number we type.

    Why it matters: It is the only price we control directly. We adjust it until the L-Price comes out right.

    Example 1: typing 68 into the B-Price box.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Joshua Radin GA row has B-Price 80; Chris Smither GA row has 57; the Alan Jackson Red Lot row has 68.

    L-Price

    The output column in SkyBox. It is the price the consumer sees on the secondary market. It is our best estimate of our net sale price, and it is the number we compare with cost to check our margin rules.

    Why it matters: Margin compliance is always checked on the L-Price, never on the B-Price.

    Example 1: B-Price 68 produces L-Price $55.00. With cost $29.36 the profit is 55 − 29.36 = $25.64.

    Example 2 (event ticket): On the Alan Jackson parking event, one ticket from the Red Lot (GA13) sold at $65.00 on 10/16 according to the Listing Statistics panel.

    Minimum L-Price

    The lowest L-Price that still meets the margin rule: cost plus the minimum profit from the chart.

    Why it matters: It is the floor. We never price below it, apart from the small wiggle room allowed for undercutting.

    Example 1: cost $35 → 35 + 27 = $62.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Alan Jackson Red Lot, cost $29.36 → 29.36 + 20 = $49.36. The actual L-Price is $55.00, so it complies.

    Zone

    A region made of sections, such as GA, Orchestra, Mezzanine, Table or Bar Seating. We compare our ticket only with tickets in the same zone.

    Why it matters: Prices differ a lot between zones, so comparing across zones would give a wrong answer.

    Example 1: a GA ticket is compared with GA tickets, not with Mezzanine.

    Example 2 (event ticket): For a GA show in Austin (e.g. Dar Williams) only the GA listings are the comparison set.

    Mapped listing

    A listing tied to a position on the venue map. It is shown on the map and is prioritised over unmapped listings when we compare.

    Why it matters: Mapped listings are the real competition; unmapped ones are less reliable.

    Rule: compare our number only with the mapped listings, not the unmapped ones. If our own listing is unmapped, compare with all listings.

    Example 1: three mapped GA listings at $84, $86, $89 and two unmapped at $60, $65: price against the mapped ones.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Alan Jackson parking: the mapped 1211 Walnut St listings ($26, $27, $28) are what we compare with.

    Unmapped listing

    A listing with no position on the venue map. It has lower priority when we compare.

    Why it matters: Its price may be misleading, so it should not set our price.

    Exception: if our own listing is unmapped, we compare with all listings, mapped and unmapped.

    Example 1: an unmapped $60 listing is ignored when mapped listings start at $84.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Alan Jackson parking: the unmapped rows at $16 and $22 are ignored.

    Undercut

    Setting our L-Price slightly below the cheapest comparable listing so that we appear first.

    Why it matters: Buyers sort by price; being cheapest gets the sale, but we undercut only as far as our margin allows.

    The detailed undercut rules are in section 9.

    Example 1: competitor at $84 → we list $83.99.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Dar Williams GA: cheapest mapped competitor $95 → we list $94.99.

    Outlier

    A price far from the rest of the zone, usually a mistake, a dump sale or a different seat type.

    Why it matters: If we copy an outlier we underprice ourselves, or we price ourselves out of the market.

    Example 1: listings at $60, $85, $86, $88: ignore the $60.

    Example 2 (event ticket): one seller shows GA at $26 while all others are $85–$95: do not undercut it.

    Wiggle room

    The dollars we may go below the regular margin: Total Profit − Sneak Profit.

    Why it matters: It tells us how far below the regular minimum we can price when the sneak margin applies.

    Example 1: cost $75: 53 − 43 = $10.

    Example 2 (event ticket): cost $113: 80 − 65 = $15. Cost $41.79: 33 − 27 = $6.

    Sneak margin

    A smaller required profit, used only when it puts our ticket in the top 5 of its zone, and never when all in pricing is in effect. See section 6.

    Why it matters: It lets us win a good position without giving up more profit than needed.

    Example 1: cost $75: regular min $128, sneak min $118.

    Example 2 (event ticket): cost $41.79 for Jon McLaughlin & Eric Hutchinson: regular min $74.79, sneak min $68.79.

    All in pricing

    The situation where SkyBox shows the total the consumer pays (including consumer fees) as the L-Price. See section 7.

    Why it matters: The displayed L-Price is then too high to compare with our margins.

    Example 1: if all in applies, cost $75 needs a price of about $150 (128 ÷ 0.85 = 150.59), not $128.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Dar Williams GA, cost $31.59, all in: price $70 (58 ÷ 0.85 = 68.24 → 68 → 70), not $58.59.

    Lone listing

    A listing with no comparable listing in its zone: we are alone in the market, so there is no market to follow and the price is ours to rule. See section 8.

    Why it matters: We then price with a fixed method based on cost.

    Example 1: cost $20 with nothing to compare with → $70.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Alan Jackson parking, a special lot with only our listing, cost $29.36 → $94.36.

    Rounding to $5

    The calculator rounds in two steps: first it drops the decimals, then it rounds up to the next multiple of 5. A number that is already a multiple of 5 stays as it is.

    Why it matters: It keeps prices clean. Because the decimals are dropped first, a price can sit up to $0.99 under the raw number, so 20.33 stays 20 and is not pushed up to 25.

    Rule: the rounded number must be higher than the cost and lower than the market. When we cannot undercut, we use this rounded number. The full calculation steps are in section 9.

    Example 1: 74.79 → drop decimals 74 → round up $75.

    Example 2: 20.33 → drop decimals 20 → stays $20 (not $25).

    Example 3 (event ticket): 94.38 → 94 → $95.

    4. Margin rules in detail

    • Margins set the minimum, not the maximum. The maximum depends on the market. If the market allows far more than the minimum, we price near the market.
    • Margins are not ironclad. It is fine to go a few dollars below the margin to undercut. How much flexibility we have depends on cost and profit.
    • Total wiggle room is the difference between the regular margin and the sneak margin.
    • Add the minimum profit to the cost to get the minimum L-Price, except when all in applies (section 7).

    Example 1: cost $47.22 → minimum 47.22 + 33 = $80.22. If the cheapest comparable GA listing is $140, we list at $139.99 and earn 139.99 − 47.22 = $92.77, not the bare minimum.

    Example 2 (event ticket): Dar Williams, Austin, GA, cost $31.59 → range $31–$40.99 → profit $27 → minimum $58.59. The cheapest mapped GA competitor is $95, so we list $94.99, a profit of $63.40.

    6. Sneak profit (Top 5 Sneak Margin), in detail

    Sneak profit is a lower profit requirement that we are allowed to use for one purpose only: to move our ticket into the top 5 of its zone. In the pricing tool, listings are sorted by L-Price from lowest to highest, so the top 5 are the five lowest-priced (best-positioned) listings. Being in the top 5 is worth a small drop in profit, because buyers see those listings first.

    Use the sneak margin only when ALL of these are true

    • Using the sneak margin will put our ticket in the top 5 of its zone.
    • Our ticket is not already in the top 5 when we use the regular margin.
    • The comparison is valid: same zone, mapped listings prioritised, outliers ignored.
    • All in pricing is not in effect. Do not use the sneak margin when all in pricing applies.

    If even one condition fails, use the regular margin. This stops us giving away profit that does not buy us anything.

    Example A, sneak applies (event ticket): Jon McLaughlin & Eric Hutchinson, GA, cost $41.79. Regular min $74.79, sneak min $68.79. Mapped GA listings in the zone: $60, $62, $64, $66, $69, $72. At $74.79 we would be 7th, so not top 5. At $68.99 (above $68.79) we undercut the $69 listing and become 5th. Profit = 68.99 − 41.79 = $27.20, which is $5.80 under the regular $33 but inside the $6 wiggle room.

    Example B, already top 5 (same ticket): the zone starts at $90, $95, $99, $105, $110. At the regular minimum $74.79 we are already #1, so the sneak margin is not needed. List near the market, e.g. $89.99, for more profit.

    Example C, sneak cannot help (event ticket): Alan Jackson Red Lot, cost $29.36. Regular min $49.36, sneak min $45.36. Mapped rivals are $26, $27 and $28, all below even the sneak minimum, so we cannot reach the top 5. Use the regular margin; the sample listing at $55.00 complies.

    Example D, general: cost $75, zone listings $100, $108, $112, $117, $119, $124. The regular min $128 would be 7th. Pricing at $118.99 (above the sneak min $118) undercuts the $119 listing and makes us 5th. Use sneak: profit $43.99, which is $9.01 under the regular $53 but inside the $10 wiggle room.

    7. All in pricing, in detail

    Normally the L-Price is a good estimate of what we net. With all in pricing, SkyBox shows the total the consumer pays as the L-Price. That total includes fees the consumer pays to SkyBox (not to us). So the displayed L-Price is higher than what we actually receive, and we cannot use it to check our margin or to compare ourselves with the market in the usual way.

    How to tell that all in is in effect

    • You are told it applies, or
    • There are visual cues: compare our L-Price with our B-Price. When the B-Price and the L-Price are close, or the B-Price is lower than the L-Price, all in pricing is in effect.
    • "Too close" is proportional. A gap of $3 is tiny on a $200 listing but large on a $20 listing.

    In the guide sample, B-Price is 68 and L-Price is $55. B is well above L, which is the normal pattern. If B were 55 and L were 54, that would suggest all in.

    How to adjust

    All in Min L-Price = (Cost + Total Profit) ÷ 0.85

    Take the calculated minimum L-Price for a non all in event (cost + total profit), drop its decimals, and divide it by 0.85. Then drop the decimals of the answer and round up to the next multiple of 5. That is the all in price. The 0.85 assumes about 15% of the displayed price goes to fees, so we keep 85%. Then undercut and/or round from this new price.

    Example 1: cost $75, all in. 75 + 53 = 128, then 128 ÷ 0.85 = 150.59, drop decimals and round up = $150. The cheapest competing (all in) listing is $170, so we list $169.99.

    Why not the regular minimum? At L = $58.59 under all in we would keep only 0.85 × 58.59 = $49.80, a profit of $18.21 instead of the required $27.

    9. The pricing process, step by step

    • 1. Determine the cost. Read the estimated cost of the listing.
    • 2. See if all in pricing is in effect. Either you are told, or you see the cue (B-Price close to the L-Price, or lower than it): then all in is in effect.
    • 3. Check the minimum profit for the cost range. Use the all in minimum if it applies. If the listing is a lone listing, use the lone listing method instead. The calculation steps are listed below.
    • 4. Compare similarly zoned tickets. Zones are regions of sections (GA, Orchestra, Mezzanine, Table, Bar Seating, etc.). Mapped listings are prioritised over unmapped ones. If our own listing is unmapped, compare with all listings.
    • 5. Input a B-Price so the L-Price is as close to the top of the similarly zoned listings as possible while still within our margins. Undercut when possible, mindful of outliers. If not, drop the decimals and round up to the next multiple of $5, which must be higher than the cost and lower than the market.

    Calculation steps (what the calculator does)

    • Value: the cost you enter (numbers only, decimals allowed).
    • Margin: the Total Profit or Sneak Profit from the chart, or the lone listing amount (cost + 50, + 65, + 85, or 2 × cost).
    • Add: Value + Margin gives the raw price, with decimals.
    • Drop the decimals: keep only the whole number (50.36 becomes 50).
    • Round up: round that whole number up to the next multiple of 5. This is the Price. A multiple of 5 stays as it is.
    • All in: take the whole number from the drop-decimals step, divide it by 0.85, drop the decimals of the answer, then round up to the next multiple of 5. This is the All in price.

    Undercut rules

    • Close market: undercut when the difference between us and the marketers is about $10 (close, with multiple marketers). We can go $15 or more higher when the market allows it, so always look at the market.
    • Between marketers: when we sit between marketers, undercut to the next higher seller minus $1.
    • Only one above us: if only one marketer is above us in the market, undercut that next higher seller.
    • Cost difference too far: if the difference between our cost and the marketers is too far, do not go all the way up to them. Use the Lone formula (section 8) instead.

    Example: marketers at $80 and $90 and our minimum is $85. We sit between them, so we list the next higher seller minus $1, which is $89.

    Other price rules

    • Same price as before: when the new L-Price number is the same as before, add 0.001 to it.
    • Price already in the market: when our L-Price is the same as one that already exists in the market, take $1 off it to compete with that marketer.
    • Multiple tickets of the same cost: get the first ticket's L-Price, then keep adding $5 for each next ticket. Inside a section, go in order from the lower quality seat to the higher quality seat.

    Example 1: the new L-Price is $95 and it was $95 before, so we enter 95.001.

    Example 2: three tickets of the same cost and the first gets $80, so the next two get $85 and $90, lower quality seat first.

    Full worked example (event ticket)

    Jon McLaughlin & Eric Hutchinson, 04 Center, Austin, Fri Jan 17, GA standing, cost $41.79.

    • Cost: $41.79.
    • All in? B-Price and L-Price are not close and nobody said it is all in → no.
    • Margin: range $41–$50.99 → $33 → minimum L = $74.79 (sneak $27 → $68.79).
    • Zone comparison: mapped GA listings sorted by L: $84, $86, $89, $92, $95. An outlier at $50 is ignored.
    • Price: the cheapest is $84, so undercut at $83.99 (above $74.79). We become #1. Profit = 83.99 − 41.79 = $42.20, above the $33 minimum. Type a B-Price, read the L-Price column, and adjust until L reads $83.99.

    If we could not undercut: with a minimum of $74.79 and no room, drop the decimals (74) and round up to the next multiple of $5, which is $75.

    If all in applied: 74.79 → 74, then 74 ÷ 0.85 = 87.06 → 87 → round up $90. Competitors are $99 and up, so list $98.99.

    If it were a lone listing: 41.79 + 85 = $126.79 → 126 → round up to $130.

    11. Formula cheat sheet and decision flow
